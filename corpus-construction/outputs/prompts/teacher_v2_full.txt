[T_PlayingByAppointment]  Playing by Appointment

  Definition:
    Requires players play at specific times (and or dates) as defined by the game, rather than the players. When mechanics result in player playing according to the schedules the game offers, rather than their personal desires (an obligation).

  Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):
    - (Alt. def.) (Hadan et al. 2024): “Patterns that forces players to adhere to the game’s schedule.”
    - Evil Battle Pass (Zhang et al.): “Players are required to spend excessive time completing tasks, risking expiration of the battle pass and loss of rewards, even if they have paid for it in advance.” [low-level]
    - Wait to Play (Hadan et al. 2024): “Patterns that impose arbitrary wait times on players through in-game timers.”
    darkpattern.games: “In-game timers that make you arbitrarily wait for something.” [low-level]
    - Trapping starter kits: This pattern particularly entices new players to log in and play continuously for the first few
    days after creating new accounts. These designs often use in-game rewards as incentives, creating
    a situation where new players lose the opportunity to obtain these rewards if they fail to log in and
    complete tasks within the specified timeframe. [dp-combo]

  Boundary rules:
    vs Non-dark scheduling:
      Zagal et al.: “Nullified if completing appointments is not required for progress. In other words, when appointment is combined with progress it becomes dark.”
    vs Fear of missing out:
      Where a reward expires on the game's clock and the span describes the schedule or the loss, code Playing by Appointment. Code FOMO only where the span describes anticipatory pressure i.e., the player continuing, returning, or feeling anxious in order not to miss out or fall behind. Both apply under R9 only if the span carries the schedule and the felt pressure.
    vs Recurring Fee:
      A paid pass or subscription alone is Recurring Fee. Assign Playing by Appointment additionally only where the span describes the pass's clock operating on the player: tasks that must be completed before expiry, rewards forfeited by not playing within the window, or pressure to play to a schedule set by the pass. Price, duration, or poor value alone do not trigger it. Where both are present, assign both under R9.
    vs Resource depletion without stated wait:
      Wait to Play requires the span to describe the wait or its regeneration schedule (lives/energy refilling over time, a build timer, a cooldown, a cooldown-skip offer).

  Worked example:

[T_MandatoryMarathon]  Mandatory Marathon

  Definition:
    Design that prevents the player from stopping at a moment of their choosing, requiring continuation to a stopping point defined by the game. Includes absent or restricted saving, checkpoint-only progress, and sessions or matches that cannot be exited without forfeiting progress.

  Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):
    - Can’t Pause or Save (Hadan et al. 2024): “Patterns that force players to continue playing before they can save progress.” [low-level]

  Boundary rules:
    vs Unskippable sequence:
      Mandatory Marathon requires that the player be prevented from ending a session or losing progress. Unskippable cutscenes, encounters, or animations within a session do not trigger it unless the review states the player could not stop playing or lost progress.

      Where a session ends through a crash, freeze, or disconnection, assign Mandatory Marathon where the same forfeit would follow a deliberate exit: the loss is the game's exit rule, which the malfunction only triggered. [M_PayToProgress]  Pay to Progress

  Definition:
    Patterns that require the players to spend money to progress in-game.

  Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):
    - Pay to Skip (Sousa & Oliveira 2023): “Permitting or actively promoting players to spend money to speed up their progress in the game.” Alt (Hadan 2024): “Patterns that allows players players to pay to bypass undesired obstacles.” Alt (Dahlan & Susanty 2022): “Allowing or even encouraging players to spend money to make progress in the game easier and faster.” darkpattern.games: “Spend money to avoid waiting for a timer to expire.” [low-level]
    - Pay to Win (Hadan et al. 2024): “Patterns that grant advantages to players who spend money.” Zagal et al.’s “Monetized Rivalries”: “Exploits Player competitiveness; encouraging them to spend money they would not otherwise in order to achieve in-game status such as a high placement on a leader board.” darkpattern.games: “A player can spend real money to purchase something that gives them an advantage in the game.” [low-level]
    - Pay Wall (Sousa & Oliveira 2023): “The game reaches a point where further progress is contingent on making a payment.” Alt (Hadan 2024): “Patterns that make the game unplayable without payment.” darkpattern.games: “The game becomes impossible to continue playing without payment.” [low-level]
    - Narrative Obligation (King et al. 2023): “the game’s narrative structure makes the purchase of in-game items a regular or repetitive part of gameplay. Although in-game currency may be earned, this is generally at a slow rate, so players may be forced to spend to keep the game flowing.” [low-level]
    - Try now, Pay later (Zhang et. al): This pattern offers players the chance to initially try premium experiences,
    powerful items, or privileges, with payment required at a later stage. [dp-combo]

  Boundary rules:
    vs Paid removal of artificial friction:
      Assign Pay to Progress when payment removes, skips, or unlocks an artificial barrier that directly blocks continued play or advancement. Friction described strictly as an annoyance or a convenience feature, with no progress consequence, does not trigger this tag. For example, paying to bypass a mandatory cooldown timer or an ad break that halts gameplay qualifies, whereas paying for expanded inventory space, cosmetic removals, or purely quality of life tweaks does not.
    vs Free alternative:
      Assign Pay to Progress when the text uses terms that imply a paid alternative, such as "free coins," "f2p," or "without paying," even if the purchase option is not explicitly named. This contrast establishes that the free path requires extra effort, meticulous play, or excessive grinding compared to a paid shortcut. The tag applies as long as the free route falls short of smooth advancement, forcing the player into a longer or more tedious process to progress. If the contrast between free and paid is mentioned without any impact on player progress, it does not trigger the label.

  Counterexample (does NOT get this label):

But its a request for the ludo team to make it free because no one ll going to pay for that. thats the concern..so it ll be easy for us to play wid friends and also we can talk...thank you!"

[M_IntermediateCurrency]  Intermediate Currency

  Definition:
    Pattern where players use real-world money to acquire in-game currency for purchases or trades within the game world

  Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):
    - Premium Currency (Sousa & Oliveira 2023): “The true cost of in-game items is concealed by the exchange rate between real-world currency and virtual currency.” Alt (Hadan 2024): “Patterns that disguises item real prices by introducing exchange rates.” darkpattern.games: “Exchange rate between real money and in-game currency disguises the real price of items.” [low-level]
    - Polymorphic Currency:is the use of multiple, purpose-specific in-game currencies within a
    single game that can either be purchased with real-world money or earned by in-game activities.
    The malicious part is that they are non-interchangeable. Zhang et al. 2025. [low-level]
    - Bulk Purchase: King et al. 2023: “the player may only purchase in-game currency in set pack sizes, with a bulk-buy discount to encourage larger purchases” [low-level]
    - Leftovers: King et al. 2023: “the pricing of items is not aligned to the pack sizes, meaning that players must often purchase more currency than required.” [low-level]

  Boundary rules:
    vs Purchases denominated in currency:
      Direction of the transaction decides it. Where the currency is the object acquired: "buy coins", "buy gems", "top up", real money is the consideration and the conversion is in the span, even if money is not named or priced. Where the currency is the means of payment: "bought with coins", "cost me 1,600 gems", the span shows spending inside the game economy and does not establish the conversion; assign only if money entering the currency is described elsewhere in the review.

[M_DeceptiveLuxury]  Deceptive Luxury

  Definition:
    Deceptive Luxury is a game design tactic that makes an item feel rare and exclusive so players will pay more for it, when the rarity was invented by the developer rather than being a real limit. The item's price reflects manufactured scarcity, not genuine value.

  Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):
    - Artificial Scarcity (Hadan et al. 2024): “Patterns that use limited-time offers to manipulate player decisions.” darkpattern.games: “Limited time offers with unnecessary urgency.” [low-level]
    - Remedy Consumption: is a pattern where players are given a second opportunity to correct
    a missed purchase of some limited, valuable in-game items. This involves intentional artificial
    scarcity for these in-game items, which compels players to expend additional effort or incur further
    costs (compared to the first time) to obtain them. Zhang et al. 2025. [low-level]
    - Rarity Level (Zhang et al.): in-game items are often designed with varying levels of rarity. For
    instance, character skins are ranked from B rank to S++ rank, with
    higher ranks featuring more intricate designs and commanding higher prices [dp-enhancer]. Mention of rarity tied to price, purchase, or manufactured scarcity implies presence of pattern.

  Boundary rules:
    vs Scarcity without monetization:
      Deceptive Luxury requires the scarcity to bear on price or purchase. Availability restrictions with no monetary consequence in the span (regional exclusives, location-gated content, low spawn rates) are not Deceptive Luxury
    vs Gambling and Reward Mania:
      Assign Gambling and Reward Mania when scarcity comes from the odds of a chance mechanic, treating the low draw rate as the wager and the payment as the cost of attempts rather than the item itself. Assign Deceptive Luxury when scarcity is built directly into the item through artificial limits like time caps, exclusivity, urgency, or dedicated rarity pricing. If a chance mechanic yields an item that also features built-in scarcity, assign both rule sets under R9 because both sources are active. Note that Deceptive Luxury still requires the item's scarcity to directly affect its price or purchase terms.

  Counterexample (does NOT get this label):

    Why not:
      Mention of rarity or tier names (mythic, legendary, S-rank, prestige) is a dp-enhancer, not a low-level indicator, and does not lift to Deceptive Luxury under R5. Require a span tying the rarity to price, purchase, or manufactured scarcity.

[M_Gambling]  Gambling

  Definition:
    Using real money to participate in a game that involves an element of luck, with the possibility of receiving a prize as a reward.

  Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):
    - Loot Boxes (Hadan 2024, “Gambling / Loot Boxes”): “Patterns that encourages players to spend money on chance-based rewards.” darkpattern.games (“Gambling / Loot Boxes”): “Spending real money to play a game of chance for a reward.” [low-level]
    - Risk Conversion: offers players an alternative way to achieve their goals, such as obtaining
    an extremely rare item, by spending a substantial amount of money to circumvent the uncertainties
    inherent in gambling mechanics. However, the actual amount players spend is significantly higher
    than if they had opted for gambling mechanics. This pattern specifically targets risk-averse players. Zhang et al. 2025 [low-level]
    - Bundle Bonus: encourages players to engage in multiple gambling attempts at once rather
    than a single one. For instance, if a player undertakes 10 in-game lottery activities at once, they
    receive an additional opportunity for free, incentivizing bulk gambling. . Zhang et al. 2025. [low-level]

  Boundary rules:
    vs Intermediate Currency:
      Where real money is converted into in-game currency and that currency is then wagered, assign both Gambling and Intermediate Currency under R9. The intervening currency layer does not nullify Gambling ("using real money" is satisfied by the funding chain), and the wagering does not absorb Intermediate Currency. Assign Gambling alone where the stake is currency earned in-game with no purchase described.
    vs Reward Mania:
      Assign Reward Mania when the text describes a chance-based delivery system like gacha, loot boxes, crates, spin wheels, or summons without explicitly mentioning a monetary stake. Assign Gambling only when the text explicitly describes spent money, a purchased stake, or a wager using paid or earned intermediate currency, rather than assuming monetization just because the mechanic usually costs money. When the text contains both the chance-based mechanic and an explicit monetary stake, assign both tags under R9: Gambling for the stake and Reward Mania for the variable reward schedule.
    vs Pay to Progress:
      Assign Gambling alone when payment is used internally within a chance mechanic, such as buying another round, retrying a bet, or protecting accumulated winnings from a loss. Assign Pay to Progress additionally only when the payment advances the player outside the wager itself, such as unlocking gated content, restoring general access to the game, or progressing beyond a roadblock.

[M_EasyToPurchase]  Easy to Purchase

    - Low Barrier: King et al. 2023 “Default To Purchase”: “instances where the player was directed to purchase in-game items or in-game currency as a default action; if they do not wish to purchase, they must opt-out.” [low-level]
    - Prompted to Buy: King et al. 2023: “if the player attempts to buy an item with insufficient credit, the user interface displays a prompt for the user to purchase additional in-game currency with real money.” [low-level]

[S_ForcedFellowship]  Forced Fellowship

  Definition:
    Forced Fellowship is a game design tactic that turns real friendships into leverage: the game rewards you for pulling friends in, then makes it costly to stop playing because quitting would let those friends down.Design that uses the player's social ties, real or in-game, as the mechanism of recruitment or retention: rewarding the player for bringing others into the game, or making disengagement costly because absence burdens teammates, guildmates, or friends.

  Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):
    - Social Pyramid Scheme (Sousa & Oliveira 2023): “Players are incentivized to invite friends to join the game and are encouraged to continue spreading the invitation to others in their social circle.” Alt (Hadan 2024): “Patterns that reward players for inviting friends.” Alt (Dahlan & Susanty 2022): “Encouraging or sometimes forcing players to invite people to join them in the game to gain in-game incentives or make progress.” darkpattern.games: “You get a bonus for inviting your friends and then they have to invite their friends.” [low-level]
    - Social Obligation / Guilds (Sousa & Oliveira 2023): “Due to a sense of responsibility towards one's friends, players may feel compelled to play the game even when they would prefer not to.” Alt (Hadan 2024): “Patterns that create a sense of obligation to avoid letting down friends in the game.” darkpattern.games: “To avoid letting down your friends, you are obligated to play when you don't want to.” [low-level]

  Boundary rules:
    vs Reciprocity:
      If the player is asked to bring NEW people into the game, code Forced Fellowship
        (Social Pyramid Scheme). If the player exchanges resources, lives, or favours with
        people ALREADY playing, code Reciprocity. If the player is described as playing
        when they'd rather not, so as not to burden others, code Forced Fellowship (Social Obligation).
    vs Coerced grouping:
      Where the span shows the player compelled into a group to obtain protection or mechanical advantage against other players, or to obtain rewards with no obligation to those group members described, Forced Fellowship does not apply.

  Counterexample (does NOT get this label):
    "Nice distraction and my alliance is filled with lovely people!"
    Why not:
      Names an in-game social group without describing recruitment pressure or obligation to keep playing; the Social Obligation indicator requires the player be shown playing when they would rather not

    Labels assigned: S_ForcedFellowship
    Why:
      Implies that the reviewer has invited their friend to play the game in order to get "more energy". Falls under Social Pyramid Scheme.

[P_RewardMania]  Reward Mania

  Definition:
    Reward structures in which the mechanism of delivery, its complexity, its unpredictability, or the out-of-game activity it demands, is what sustains engagement, rather than the value of the reward itself.   Indicators (each is an instance of this pattern; see R5):

    and engaging with them. [low-level]
    - Sycophant (Zhang et al.): “Sycophant” refers to the use of rewards within the game to encourage players to allocate
    additional attention and resources to social activities outside of the game, such as following the

    - Variable Rewards (Sousa & Oliveira 2023): “Irregular or unexpected rewards have a greater potential to generate addiction compared to rewards provided on a predictable schedule.” Alt (Hadan 2024): “Patterns that offer unpredictable or random rewards to enhance game addiction.” darkpattern.games: “Unpredictable or random rewards are more addictive than a predictable schedule.” [low-level]
    - Gacha mechanics like pulls, rolls, banners. [indicators]
